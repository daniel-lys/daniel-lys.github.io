The short version

Puzzle Play has no accounts, no sign-in, and no purchases. Your photos, the puzzles you keep and anything a parent writes stay on the phone and are never uploaded.

The app is free because it shows ads, supplied by Google AdMob. That is the only part of the app that uses the internet and the only place third parties are involved. Because the app is registered with Google as directed to children, those ads are not personalised: no profile is built, no interests are used, and the Android advertising ID is not collected at all — the permission for it has been removed from the app.

To deliver an ad, Google does receive some technical information: the device's IP address, whether an ad was shown or tapped, and basic diagnostic data. The Advertising section sets out exactly what, and the Summary table matches, line for line, what is declared in the app's Data safety section on Google Play.

Who this policy is for

Puzzle Play is made for young children, and is declared on Google Play with a target audience of children. It therefore falls under Google Play's Families policy, the US Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA), and — for children in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom and Switzerland — the GDPR and UK GDPR.

This policy is written to be read by a parent or guardian. Where it says “you”, it means the adult responsible for the device.

What the app does with your photos

When you tap “Pick a photo”, Android's own system photo picker opens. The app never sees your photo library: the picker runs outside the app, you choose one picture, and Android hands the app temporary, read-only access to just that single picture.

The app does not hold — and has never requested — the READ_MEDIA_IMAGES or READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE permission. It has no way to browse, list or read your gallery.

With the picture it has been handed, the app:

  1. reads it into memory,
  2. cuts it into puzzle pieces in memory,
  3. shows it on screen.

The picture is never transmitted anywhere. It exists only in the app's memory while you are playing and is gone when the app closes — with one exception, which happens only if you ask for it: see “Puzzles you choose to keep”.

What is stored on the device

A handful of small preferences, in the app's private storage, which no other app can read:

StoredWhy
Sound on or offSo the app remembers the choice
Chosen difficultySo the app opens on the level last played
Chosen languageSo the app opens in the language picked
A name, typed on first launchSo “My puzzles” can be labelled with it instead of a generic title
Whether the daily reminder is on, and the hour it is set toSo the app knows whether to schedule it, and for when
The run of days, the best run, and how many puzzles have been finishedSo the app can show “3 days in a row”

The first three are kept without you doing anything. The name is asked once, on first launch, and answering is optional — tapping through the empty field is a complete answer, and the question is not asked again.

That name is the only piece of personal information the app holds. It is stored on the device and nowhere else: it is never transmitted, never passed to the ad system, and never used to identify anyone. You can change it or clear it at any time from “My puzzles”. Uninstalling the app deletes all of it.

The run of days is four numbers — a date, two counts and a total. It is not a history of when the app was used: only the most recent day is kept, so there is no record of which days were which. Nothing about it is sent anywhere.

The daily reminder

The app can show one notification a day, at an hour a grown-up picks in Settings — six in the evening until it is changed — and only on a day when no puzzle has been finished. It is off unless a grown-up turns it on, and that switch is also the only place Android's notification permission is ever asked for — never at launch, and never in front of a child.

The reminder is scheduled entirely on the device. Nothing is sent to any server to make it happen, no push service is involved, and the app has no way of knowing whether it was seen. Turning the switch off cancels it immediately. So that a reminder survives the phone being restarted — a reboot clears every alarm the system was holding — the app also asks Android to tell it when the phone has started up. That is a silent permission you are never prompted for, and it is used for nothing else.

It never says a run is about to end and never counts down, because a reminder built on the fear of losing something is not a reminder a young child should be given.

Puzzles you choose to keep

When you finish a puzzle, the victory screen offers a tick box: “Keep this one in my puzzles”. It is off every time. Nothing below happens unless you tick it.

StoredWhy
The date and time you finishedSo “My puzzles” can show when it was played
How long the round tookSo times on the same puzzle can be compared
The grid and number of piecesSo the puzzle can be rebuilt at the same size
A resized copy of the pictureSo the puzzle can be played again

The copy of the picture is written into the app's private storage. It is not placed in your gallery, not indexed by other apps, and never leaves the phone.

You can remove any of it at any time: “My puzzles” has a delete button on each puzzle, which deletes its saved picture along with its times, and a “Delete all” button that empties everything. Uninstalling the app removes it all as well.

Puzzles made from the pictures that ship with the app store no image at all — those are drawn by the app from scratch each time.

The film studio

Three deliberate taps on the mascot, and a small sum only a grown-up will answer, lead to a corner of the app that makes a short video out of words and, if you like, one of your photos. It is not on any menu, and a child tapping around the home screen does not fall into it.

The whole film is made on the phone, with Android's own video encoder. Nothing is uploaded, no service is contacted, and no copy is kept anywhere but on your device. Two buttons there can put a file outside the app, and each takes a deliberate tap:

  • Save to gallery writes the finished MP4 into your device's own video library, the same place a camera app would put it. It stays on the phone.
  • Share hands that one file to whichever app you pick in Android's share sheet, one file at a time, under a temporary grant that Android manages. Where it goes after that is between you and the app you chose — Puzzle Play plays no part in it and is not told what you picked.

Leaving the studio without doing either keeps nothing: the film exists only while you are making it.

Puzzles a grown-up prepares

“My puzzles” has a + button leading to a corner meant for a parent: pick a picture, choose how many pieces, and write the note the child finds when the last piece lands. Puzzles can be sorted into folders, and a folder can be given a password.

StoredWhy
The folder nameSo the shelf has a label
The note, and where on the picture it sitsSo it appears where you put it
A resized copy of the pictureSo the puzzle can be played
A digest of the folder passwordSo a typed password can be checked

About the password. It is never stored. What is stored is a PBKDF2-SHA256 digest with a random salt, which can confirm a word someone types without revealing what that word is. Be clear about what this lock does and does not do:

  • It does keep a child from opening that folder inside the app.
  • It does not encrypt the puzzles, pictures or notes inside. They are ordinary files in the app's private storage. Anyone with the unlocked phone and developer tools could read them, exactly as before the lock existed.
  • It cannot be recovered. Nothing in the app can tell the person who set the password from anyone else, so a forgotten password means the folder can only be deleted, with its contents.

None of this leaves the phone.

If you have Android's own backup enabled in your Google account, Android may include the small settings file in your device backup. That backup is handled by Google under your own Google account settings; the app has no part in it and no access to it.

Advertising

The app shows ads through Google AdMob (Google Ireland Limited for users in the EEA and Switzerland, Google LLC elsewhere). Two kinds, and no others:

WhereWhat
Between roundsA full-screen ad, at most once every two puzzles. Never when the app opens, never during a puzzle, never over the restart button. It can be closed.
When the magic pieces run outAn offer to watch a video in exchange for one more piece. It appears only if the spent wand is tapped, it is refusable, and the video can be closed early — you simply do not get the extra piece.

No ad is ever required in order to play. If Google has no ad to serve, the round starts anyway — there is never a video to watch in order to continue, and nothing in the app is held back until an ad has been shown.

The whole app works offline. Choosing a photo, cutting it into pieces, playing, keeping the result, and everything a grown-up has prepared: none of it needs a connection. Ads are the only thing that does, and when there is none they are simply skipped.

Child-directed mode

The app is registered with Google as directed to children, and every ad request is tagged accordingly. That changes what AdMob is permitted to do:

  • No personalised advertising. No profile, no interests and no browsing history are used to choose what is shown.
  • No interest-based advertising and no remarketing.
  • No advertising ID. The identifier Android normally gives apps for tracking people between apps is not collected, and the AD_ID permission has been removed from the app altogether.
  • Only ads from Google Play Families self-certified ad sources are eligible, and their content is limited to the “G” rating.

Google's advertising partners

Google does not fill every ad on its own. An ad request is offered to a set of advertising technology partners — the companies that buy advertising space — and the data listed in the table above may be shared with them so they can decide whether to bid and what to show. This app uses Google's commonly used set of partners, which numbers in the low hundreds and changes over time as Google updates it.

To be plain about what that means: the technical data above is seen by more than Google alone. What does not change is everything else on this page — no advertising ID is collected, no personalised advertising is served, no profile is built, and nothing that identifies your child is sent to anyone.

Consent and your choices

For users in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom and Switzerland, the app uses Google's User Messaging Platform — a Google-certified consent management platform — to handle consent for advertising.

Because the audience is children, personalised advertising is switched off unconditionally in this app, everywhere in the world. It is not a setting, and it does not depend on anyone agreeing to anything: no profile is built, no interests are used, and the advertising ID is not collected.

That is also why the app does not put a consent dialog in front of a child. Every request is tagged as coming from a user under the age of consent, which tells Google's consent platform not to ask. There is no personalised advertising to consent to, and asking a young child to make a decision of that kind would not produce a meaningful answer.

Where your region nonetheless provides for a standing choice, the app shows it under Settings → Privacy → “Ad privacy choices”, in the grown-up corner. It is there whenever the law asks for it, and absent where it would open an empty form.

You can also, at the device level and independently of this app:

  • Reset or delete the Android advertising ID under Settings → Privacy → Ads (this app does not collect it in any case).
  • Uninstall the app, which removes everything it has stored.

What the app does not do

  • It requests four Android permissions and no others, and only one of them is ever put to you as a question: internet access, needed to fetch an ad; permission to see whether the phone has a connection; permission to show notifications, which is the one you are asked for, and only if a grown-up switches the daily reminder on; and permission to be told the phone has restarted, so that a reminder you switched on is still there after a reboot. Not storage, not photos, not camera, not microphone, not location, not contacts, not the advertising ID. You can verify this on the app's Google Play listing, under “App permissions”.
  • It contains no analytics SDK and no crash-reporting SDK. The only third-party code in the app is the Google Mobile Ads SDK described above.
  • It never transmits your photos, your saved puzzles, the notes a parent writes, or any name typed into the app.
  • It creates no accounts and asks for no email address, phone number or password of yours.
  • It does not read the device phone number, the IMEI, the MAC address, the SIM serial, or any other persistent hardware identifier.
  • It does not use the microphone or the camera. Sounds are generated by the app itself; there is no recording of any kind.
  • It contains no purchases. Nothing in the app costs money.
  • It contains no social features, no chat, no accounts and no way for anyone to reach anyone else through it. Nothing is published, and no content is uploaded to us or to anybody — there is no server to upload it to. The one thing a grown-up can send out of the app is a film they made in the studio, to an app they choose themselves; see “The film studio” above.
  • The only links out of the app are this policy, wherever an ad leads if it is tapped, and Android's own share sheet if the studio is used.

How long things are kept, and how to delete them

On the device. Preferences, saved puzzles, pictures and notes are kept until you delete them or uninstall the app. There is no expiry and no automatic upload. Deleting a puzzle in “My puzzles” removes its picture with it; “Delete all” empties everything; uninstalling removes the lot.

At Google and its ad partners. The advertising data listed above is retained by Google, and by the advertising partners it is shared with, under their own retention schedules — Google's are described in the Google privacy terms for partner sites and apps, and each partner's are in its own privacy policy, linked from the partner list above. Because no advertising ID or account identifier is collected from this app, that data cannot be traced back to an individual child by us, and we hold no copy of it.

Because the app stores nothing on any server of ours, there is no account to close and no data of yours for us to delete on request. If you would like confirmation of that in writing, or have any other question, write to the address below and you will get a reply.
